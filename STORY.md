# The Klemm Story — Best-Case Scenarios

Written 2026-08-30, pre-launch. A fun look at what "everything goes right" could look like.
Version 1 is the grounded best case. Version 2 turns the dial up and brings in the family.
Version 3 is the realistic one — luck and hard work, real friction, real money.

---

## Version 1: Extended Best-Case Edition

**This week.** Saturday and Sunday you polish. Monday, the serendipity: Jack's title rep knows a domain guy, the exact URL Jack wanted is sitting there unregistered, and he snags it for the price of a lunch. Tuesday he sends the last headshot, two testimonials, and — unprompted — a shoebox of old newsletters going back to 2009, because "you said people might like these." Wednesday you set up the analytics property, paste over the G- code, and tracking gets wired into every page. Thursday, full walkthrough on both your phones. Jack keeps tapping his own phone number on the contact page and grinning when his phone rings. That's four phone taps in the data before launch. You leave them in. They become a running joke — every monthly report forever after has a footnote: *"minus Jack testing."*

**Friday. Launch.** Jack does what a guy with 25 years of relationships can do: one email to his entire sphere, posts everywhere, new email signature, QR codes ordered for his sign riders. The email subject line is pure Jack: "I finally got a website. My kids are shocked."

**Launch weekend.** 340 visits in 48 hours. You refresh the realtime view so many times your wife asks if you're okay. Sunday at 9:40pm, the Del Webb page suddenly does a vertical line — 31 people on it at once. You text Jack in a mild panic thinking something's broken. Nothing's broken. A woman named Diane in the Woodbridge community Facebook group posted the page with the caption "FINALLY someone wrote down how this place actually works," and retired people, it turns out, are extremely online on Sunday nights. Diane has never met Jack. By Tuesday she's referred him a neighbor who's downsizing. Diane becomes a recurring character. Every time Del Webb traffic spikes, you text Jack one word: *"Diane."*

**September — 1,200 visits.** The launch spike settles into a real pattern and the first report lands the famous line: **a third of his visitors are physically sitting in the Bay Area.** Jack had said it for years at listing appointments — "your buyer is coming over the Altamont" — but now he prints the little geography table and slides it across sellers' kitchen tables. He starts calling it "the Altamont slide." Two sellers later tell him it's why they picked him over the discount brokerage. Meanwhile the quirky data: someone in Reykjavik read the entire past-sales page. Someone visits the Lathrop page every single Tuesday at lunch, same time, like clockwork. You and Jack name him "Lathrop Larry" and speculate wildly. The average read time on the newsletters page is over four minutes — longest on the site — which stuns Jack, who'd argued the page was "filler for the three people who care." It's not three people. It's his entire past-client list, reading every word.

**October — Google wakes up.** The city pages start ranking for the searches no big portal bothers with — "Del Webb Woodbridge resale," "River Islands agent," "Mountain House schools homes." Almost no local agent has actual *written pages* about these communities; they have a Zillow headshot and a prayer. Organic search becomes his second-biggest traffic source. Then the milestone: a Mountain House buyer finds the page cold from Google, reads it, taps the phone number. No referral, no sign, no history. Jack can see the whole path in the data — Google → Mountain House page → phone tap → signed buyer. First provable internet-born client of his career. He tells the story at his office meeting. His broker asks who built the site. Remember that broker.

**November — the flywheel.** 2,500 visits. River Islands and Manteca are clearly the engines, so you both lean in: Jack films a slightly awkward, entirely charming walkthrough of a River Islands model home ("they tell me this is called a great room, and folks, it is great"), you post it, page traffic doubles. Lathrop Larry finally converts — he was a Tesla-commuting renter timing the market, and he'd been reading that page every Tuesday for three months working up the nerve. Jack closes him in December. You frame nothing, but you *do* screenshot the Tuesday pattern. Quarterly tally: ~130 phone taps, 35 form submits, two signed listings, one Larry. The site cost a domain name and your time.

**December — the broker calls.** Jack's broker wants "whatever Jack has" for the whole office. You politely decline the whole office — you're not running an agency — but the ask itself changes something. You realize the report is a product. You tighten the monthly one-pager into a thing of beauty, and Jack starts bringing it to listing appointments like a closing exhibit.

**January — the granular gold strike.** Deep in the data you notice something weird: the Manteca page gets a burst of traffic every time a certain employer in the Bay Area has a layoff-slash-relocation announcement in the news. People are researching where to land, and Jack's plain-English city page keeps catching them. You jump on it — a small update to the Manteca and Lathrop pages speaking directly to relocating families, a newsletter issue titled "Moving over the hill: what nobody tells you." That issue gets forwarded so many times it briefly out-traffics the homepage. Jack picks up four relocation buyers in six weeks off pages you tuned because of one squiggle in a CSV. This becomes the pattern: the analytics show a squiggle, you two have a ten-minute call, something small changes on the site, money falls out. Jack starts calling your monthly call "the squiggle meeting."

**February — the buddy.** Jack's college roommate Dale sells lake homes in Coeur d'Alene, Idaho. Dale has been listening to Jack talk about "his web guy" for five months and finally cracks: "Whatever Jack's paying you, fine." You build Dale a site with the same bones — honest community pages, real newsletters, no chest-beating — but a totally different feel, because Dale is a fleece-vest lake guy and Jack is a Central Valley handshake guy, and the sites should know that. Dale's launch does the Del Webb thing all over again, except it's a lake association forum instead of a Facebook group and the Diane is named Chuck.

**March–April — it gets a name.** Dale's site works. Dale talks. Coeur d'Alene agents talk to Boise agents. Meanwhile back home, Jack has his best Q1 in a decade and — this is the part he repeats to everyone — *he can show exactly why*. At a regional conference in Sacramento, Jack does a ten-minute panel slot about it, puts the Altamont slide and a squiggle chart on the projector, and ends with "my guy does the sites and the numbers, no I will not give you his number, okay fine, see me after." Eleven agents see him after. You take three — the ones whose markets have the same shape: overlooked communities that no one's bothered to actually write about. You quietly notice that's your niche now. Not "websites." *Places that deserve a real page, for agents who actually know them.*

**May — you have a decision to make.** You've got Jack humming, Dale humming, three builds in flight, a waitlist you never advertised, and a monthly reporting routine so smooth it takes 15 minutes per client. Jack's business is up enough that he does something ridiculous and very Jack: he sends you a gift basket with a printed screenshot of the analytics dashboard laminated inside, signed like a jersey. Diane's neighbor's downsize closed. Larry's housewarming invite is on your fridge. And Dale's broker — who covers three states — wants a meeting.

## Version 2: Everything Turns Out Good — The Family Edition

**This week.** Saturday you polish. Sunday, Margaret wanders past your desk, looks at the sell page over your shoulder, and says the staging photos are "fine." You know that tone. Meg used to shoot — really shoot — and "fine" from her means *I could do better in my sleep*. You file that away. Monday, the domain serendipity: Jack's title rep knows a guy, the exact URL is unregistered, Jack grabs it for lunch money. Tuesday, Jack sends the last assets plus a shoebox of newsletters going back to 2009. Wednesday, the analytics property goes up and tracking gets wired into every page. Thursday's walkthrough: Jack taps his own phone number four times just to feel his pocket buzz. The footnote *"minus Jack testing"* is born and never dies.

**Friday. Launch.** Jack emails his entire sphere — subject line: "I finally got a website. My kids are shocked." — and the counter starts spinning. 340 visits by Sunday. And Sunday at 9:40pm: the Del Webb vertical line. Diane. The Woodbridge Facebook group. "FINALLY someone wrote down how this place actually works." Thirty-one retirees on one page at once. By Tuesday, Diane — who has never met Jack — has referred him her downsizing neighbor. You text Jack one word every time that page spikes for the rest of recorded history: *"Diane."*

**September.** 1,300 visits, and the first monthly one-pager lands the Altamont slide: a third of Jack's visitors sitting in the Bay Area, in a printed table he now slides across sellers' kitchen tables like evidence. Also in the data: Reykjavik reads the past-sales page, and Lathrop Larry begins his every-Tuesday-at-lunch ritual. But the September twist is at home. You show Meg the site traffic, and she shows you Jack's current listing photos and says the quiet part out loud: "These are killing him. Let me shoot one." She dusts off the camera bag. Jack, who trusts you completely now, hands her a vacant listing on a Thursday. The photos come back and they are not "fine." They're the kind where the light in the great room looks like it costs extra. The listing gets 3x the page views of any listing before it and two offers in a weekend. Jack calls the house line — the house line — to ask if "the photographer" is available for all of them. Meg is officially back.

**October.** Google wakes up, exactly on schedule. The city pages start owning the long-tail searches — Del Webb resale, River Islands agent, Mountain House schools — and organic becomes the number-two source. The first cold internet client arrives: Google → Mountain House page → phone tap → signed buyer, the whole path visible in the data. Meanwhile, a production problem becomes a family solution: Meg's listing shoots look great empty, but the lifestyle shots — the ones that make a house feel like a Saturday morning — need a human in them. Enter Dylan, who needs exactly zero convincing to stand in a $900k kitchen holding a coffee mug looking pensively toward a window. He's a natural. Reading on a porch swing, walking a hallway, "casually" gardening. The staged-with-Dylan listings consistently out-view the empty ones, and now you have a chart proving your own kid is a conversion asset. Dylan asks if he can list "residential lifestyle model" on his socials. You allow it.

**November.** 2,800 visits. The River Islands walkthrough video ("folks, it is *great*") doubles the page. Lathrop Larry converts after twelve consecutive Tuesdays — turns out he's a renter who'd been working up his nerve, and Jack closes him before Christmas. And the workload officially outgrows you: three of Jack's listings live at once, Meg shooting, monthly reports due, and Dale — Jack's college roommate in Coeur d'Alene — already sniffing around. So you do the thing you'd been circling for a month: you bring in Cal. Real job, real pay. He takes over the monthly data pull — downloads the four CSVs, files them by month, flags the squiggles before your call with Jack. Within two reports he's catching things you missed; he's the one who notices the Manteca page pulsing in sync with Bay Area relocation news. The January gold strike? That's Cal's squiggle. The relocation-tuned pages and the "Moving over the hill" newsletter it inspires bring Jack four buyers in six weeks. Jack starts addressing the monthly email to "the department."

**December–January.** Jack's best winter ever, and he can prove precisely why, which he mentions to absolutely everyone. His broker asks for "whatever Jack has" for the office; you decline the office and keep the family shop. The one-pager gets beautiful. Meg's photos start getting reshared by the builders themselves — River Islands' own marketing account reposts one of her twilight shots, credits her, and her inquiry inbox stops being theoretical. Christmas card this year: the four of you in front of a sold sign, shot on a tripod, Dylan doing the pensive-window look ironically. Jack frames one for his office.

**February.** Dale cracks: "Whatever Jack's paying you, fine." The Coeur d'Alene build becomes the first true family production — you on design and build, Cal setting up the analytics and the reporting template from day one, Meg flying up for a three-day shoot of lake communities in the snow (the photos are absurd; Idaho in February looks like a movie), Dylan in a flannel on a dock because of course. Dale's launch does the Del Webb thing all over again via a lake association forum, and Dale's Diane is named Chuck. Two months in, Dale's numbers are pacing ahead of Jack's launch curve, which Cal points out in a chart, which Jack pretends to be furious about.

**March–April.** The Sacramento conference. Jack's ten-minute panel slot becomes the talk of the hallway: the Altamont slide, Cal's squiggle chart, Meg's before-and-after photos on the projector, and Jack's closer — "my family guy and his actual family do the whole thing, no I will not give you their number, okay fine, see me after." *Fourteen* agents see him after. You take four, all the same shape: overlooked communities, agents who genuinely know them. The niche now has a waitlist and the waitlist has a spreadsheet, which Cal maintains, because Cal maintains the spreadsheets now. A regional real estate podcast has Jack on; he spends half the episode talking about the newsletters page read times. Word does what word does.

**May.** Inventory of the absurd: Jack's pipeline is the fullest of his career and every month a one-pager proves the site's fingerprints on it. Dale's broker — three states — wants a meeting, and this time you take it, because you have a photographer, an analyst, a lifestyle model, and a system. Meg's shooting for two builders directly now, on her own invoice. Cal's talking about whether the reporting could be its own product. Dylan has been recognized in public exactly once, at a Target in Manteca, by a woman who said "you're the porch swing kid," and he will never recover and neither will you. Diane's neighbor closed. Larry hosts a housewarming; the whole family goes. Jack sends the gift basket with the laminated dashboard signed like a jersey, but this year there are four signatures' worth of thanks on the card.

Late one Friday, the phone rings. Dale's broker. "So. Three states. What would it take?"

You look at Meg. Meg looks at the camera bag.

## Version 3: The Realistic One — Luck, Grind, and Real Money

**This week.** The URL takes until Wednesday, not Monday — Jack's first choice is parked by a squatter asking $2,400, so he takes the .homes variant for $30 and stops caring about it within a day, which is very Jack. Thursday you set up the analytics together over a screen share that takes forty minutes because Google makes him verify his identity twice. Friday the site launches quietly. No fireworks. Jack sends his sphere email Saturday morning instead because "people read email on Saturdays." He's right, annoyingly.

**Launch weekend.** 260 visits. Not 340 — 260, and about 30 of those are you, Jack, and Jack's wife on three devices. Still: the Del Webb thing happens. Smaller than the fantasy version — 9 people on the page at once, not 31 — but Diane is real, the Facebook group is real, and the downsizing neighbor referral is real. It just takes three weeks to turn into a phone call instead of three days. That's the pattern for everything that follows: the wins are real, they're just on a lag.

**September.** 700 visits, not 1,200. The first monthly report takes you two hours to build because it's the first one; by December it takes twenty minutes. The Altamont slide lands exactly as hoped — 28% of visitors in the Bay Area — and Jack starts printing it for listing appointments. One seller visibly does not care. Another one hires him partly because of it and says so. Batting .500 on a new pitch is a great average. Phone taps for the month: 14. Form submits: 4. One of the four is spam from an SEO company offering to improve the site's SEO. You frame that one mentally.

**October.** Google barely notices the site yet — organic search is 40 visits all month. This is normal and you knew it, but knowing it doesn't make the graph less flat. What carries October is Jack himself: he mentions the site at two open houses, adds the QR code to his sign riders, and the direct traffic stays healthy. Meg shoots her first listing for him — she's rusty for the first hour and then she is absolutely not — and Jack pays her actual market rate, $350, unprompted, because Jack pays people. The listing does noticeably better than his previous ones. Whether that's the photos, the price, or the market, nobody can prove. Everybody quietly believes it's the photos.

**November.** The lag starts paying. Organic search triples off its tiny base — the Del Webb and River Islands pages crack the first page of Google for a handful of hyper-specific searches, because nobody else wrote real pages about these places. Lathrop Larry exists, sort of: not a Tuesday-lunch cartoon, but the data does show a repeat visitor pattern on the Lathrop page, and one of the month's form submits mentions "I've been reading your Lathrop page for a while." That client closes in January. Commission on that one deal: more than Jack has ever spent on marketing in any year of his career. He does that math out loud on the phone to you, twice.

**December–February, the honest middle.** Winter is slow in real estate and the traffic shows it. There's a stretch in January where the numbers dip and you catch yourself wondering if October was the peak. It wasn't — it's just seasonality, and February proves it — but this is the part the fantasy versions skip: two months where the report says "basically flat" and the work is just keeping the newsletters going and tuning pages. Cal starts helping here, a few paid hours a month, pulling the CSVs and building the month-over-month comparison, mostly because you're busy and he's careful. The relocation squiggle shows up in February — Manteca page traffic pulsing with Bay Area layoff news — and the page updates you make off it become the single best decision of the year, though you won't know that until May.

**March.** The spring market opens and everything compounds at once: seasonal demand, six months of Google trust, tuned pages, and Jack's mouth. 2,100 visits. Jack has nine active clients and can trace four of them to the site — two from Google, one from Diane's group, one from a newsletter forward. His spring quarter ends up his best in six years. He gives you a bonus you didn't invoice for and won't take no on. Dale calls in April, not February — Jack had been telling the story for months, but Dale, like all real estate agents, only acts when he's losing listings, and in April he loses one to an agent with a good website. You quote Dale real money — $6,500 for the build plus $400 a month for hosting, upkeep, and the report — and brace for pushback. Dale says "fine" so fast you realize you underpriced it.

**April–June.** The Dale build teaches you the second-site lesson: it goes twice as fast because you've done it once, and it's still a lot of work because every place is different. Meg flies up for the shoot — her invoice, her rate. Dylan's porch-swing career consists of exactly two photos, both great, both his idea. Dale launches in June, does his own version of the sphere email, and his numbers track Jack's launch curve closely enough that Cal's comparison chart looks like a template. That chart becomes your sales deck, basically. Two agents from Jack's conference chatter reach out over the summer. You take one — a Discovery Bay agent whose market has the exact shape you now know how to serve — and waitlist the other, which feels insane to say out loud and turns out to be the most persuasive thing you could have done.

**By the one-year mark.** Three client sites live, one in the pipeline, roughly $2,000 a month in recurring retainer income on top of build fees, Meg with a photography sideline that pays for itself several times over, Cal with a real ongoing role, and a reporting system that takes an evening a month total. Jack's year: his best gross commission income in a decade, with a paper trail connecting a meaningful slice of it to a website that cost him four figures and some newsletters. He refers everyone and screens them first — "I only send you the good ones" — which makes him, functionally, your unpaid head of sales. Nothing exploded. Nothing went viral. It was luck you were positioned for, a niche nobody else bothered with, and fourteen months of Tuesdays.

The Friday call from Dale's broker still comes. In this version you say, "Let me check the calendar."

**The end — for now.**
